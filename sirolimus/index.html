What is sirolimus?

Sirolimus (also known as rapamycin; brand name in Japan: Rapalimus) is a medicine that inhibits a protein called mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin). mTOR acts as a control center inside cells, regulating cell growth and the formation of blood and lymphatic vessels. Sirolimus was originally developed as an immunosuppressant, but it is now also used as a molecular targeted drug for several rare diseases, including intractable vascular anomalies.

Why does it work for vascular anomalies?

Research over the past two decades has shown that many vascular anomalies are caused by gene variants that over-activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in the cells lining blood and lymphatic vessels. This over-activation drives the abnormal growth of vessels. By blocking mTOR, sirolimus suppresses this abnormal signaling.

Since around 2010, clinical experience worldwide has accumulated showing that sirolimus can shrink vascular tumors, improve the Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon, and reduce symptoms such as pain, bleeding, and lymphatic leakage in many patients with intractable vascular anomalies. It does not usually eliminate a malformation completely — the goal of treatment is to control symptoms and improve quality of life.

Approval in Japan

In Japan, sirolimus (Rapalimus tablets) was approved for intractable lymphatic anomalies (lymphangioma / lymphatic malformation, lymphangiomatosis, Gorham–Stout disease, and lymphangiectasia) in 2021, based on investigator-initiated clinical trials in which our research group played a central role.

In January 2024, the indication was expanded to cover intractable vascular tumors and vascular malformations — including kaposiform hemangioendothelioma and related vascular tumors, venous malformations (including blue rubber bleb nevus syndrome), and combined vascular malformations such as Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome. At the same time, a granule formulation was approved, making it easier for infants and small children to take the medicine.

The approval in Japan was supported by the SIVA trials — multicenter, investigator-initiated clinical trials evaluating the efficacy and safety of sirolimus for intractable vascular tumors and vascular malformations — conducted by our research group together with hospitals across Japan.

Approval status, brand names, and covered indications differ between countries. If you live outside Japan, please ask your physician about the situation in your country.

How sirolimus is used

  • Oral medicine, taken daily. Sirolimus is taken by mouth once a day, usually over a long period.
  • Blood level monitoring. The amount of sirolimus in the blood (trough concentration) is measured regularly, and the dose is adjusted to keep it within the target range. This keeps the treatment effective while reducing side effects.
  • Regular check-ups. Blood tests and clinical examinations are performed regularly to monitor both the effect of treatment and possible side effects.
  • Specialist supervision. Treatment should be started and managed by physicians experienced in vascular anomalies and in the use of sirolimus.

Side effects and safety

Common or important side effects include:

  • Mouth ulcers (stomatitis) — the most common side effect.
  • Increased susceptibility to infection, because sirolimus mildly suppresses the immune system.
  • Abnormal blood test results, such as high cholesterol or triglycerides, and changes in blood cell counts.
  • Interstitial lung disease — rare but important; the risk may increase with higher blood concentrations of the drug.
  • Other effects such as acne-like rash, delayed wound healing, and irregular menstruation.

Live vaccines generally should not be given during treatment, and sirolimus interacts with several other medicines and with grapefruit juice — always tell your medical team about everything you are taking. Most side effects can be managed by dose adjustment and regular monitoring. Never change the dose or stop the medicine on your own; always consult your doctor first.

Frequently asked questions

Will sirolimus cure my vascular malformation?

Sirolimus usually controls symptoms and reduces the size or activity of lesions rather than eliminating them. Symptoms may return if the medicine is stopped, so the treatment plan — including how long to continue — should be discussed with your doctor.

Can children take sirolimus?

Yes. Many of the patients in the Japanese clinical trials were children, and the granule formulation approved in 2024 was developed to make the medicine easier for infants and young children to take. Dosing in children is carefully adjusted using body size and blood level monitoring.

How do I know if sirolimus is an option for me or my child?

The first step is an accurate diagnosis at a facility experienced in vascular anomalies. Whether sirolimus is appropriate depends on the type of anomaly, the symptoms, and the treatments already tried. Please consult a specialist center.
