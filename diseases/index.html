How vascular anomalies are classified

Vascular anomalies are classified internationally according to the ISSVA classification (International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies). The most important distinction is between vascular tumors, in which the cells lining blood vessels proliferate abnormally, and vascular malformations, which are structural abnormalities of blood or lymphatic vessels that are present from birth and grow with the child.

Vascular tumorsVascular malformations
Nature Proliferation of vascular cells (mostly benign, e.g. infantile hemangioma) Structural malformation of capillaries, lymphatics, veins, or arteries
Typical course Often appear after birth, grow rapidly, then many regress spontaneously Present at birth (though not always visible), grow in proportion to the body, do not regress
Examples Infantile hemangioma, kaposiform hemangioendothelioma (KHE), tufted angioma Capillary, lymphatic, venous, and arteriovenous malformations; combined types (e.g. Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome)

Because the terms used in daily practice (such as “hemangioma”) have historically been applied to many different conditions, an accurate diagnosis by physicians familiar with vascular anomalies is the essential first step toward appropriate treatment.

Infantile hemangioma and other vascular tumors

Infantile hemangioma (“strawberry mark”)

Infantile hemangioma is the most common vascular tumor, appearing in the first weeks of life as a red, raised, strawberry-like mark. It typically grows rapidly during the first months, then slowly shrinks over several years. Many need no treatment, but lesions near the eyes, airway, or lips, ulcerated lesions, or very large lesions may need early treatment. Oral propranolol is the standard first-line drug therapy in such cases, so early referral to a specialist is important.

Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma (KHE) and tufted angioma

These are rare vascular tumors that can be locally aggressive. KHE may be complicated by the Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon, in which platelets are consumed within the tumor and severe bleeding tendency develops — a condition that requires urgent, specialized care. Drug therapies including sirolimus have been reported to reduce tumor size and improve the Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon.

Capillary malformation

Capillary malformation (also called “port-wine stain”) is a flat, red-to-purple patch of skin caused by malformed capillaries. It is present at birth and persists throughout life, sometimes darkening or thickening with age. Laser therapy is the main treatment for the skin lesions. Capillary malformations of certain distributions can be associated with syndromes (for example Sturge–Weber syndrome), so evaluation by a specialist is recommended.

Lymphatic malformation

Lymphatic malformation (formerly called “lymphangioma”) is a malformation of lymphatic vessels forming cysts filled with lymph fluid. It commonly involves the head, neck, or axilla, but can occur anywhere, including the chest and abdomen. Macrocystic lesions are often treated with sclerotherapy, while microcystic or extensive lesions can be difficult to treat and may cause swelling, infection, pain, lymph leakage, or breathing and swallowing problems.

Extensive lymphatic anomalies — such as generalized lymphatic anomaly (GLA), Gorham–Stout disease, and kaposiform lymphangiomatosis (KLA) — involve many organs or bones and are among the most intractable conditions in this field. Sirolimus has become an important treatment option for these intractable lymphatic anomalies and is approved in Japan for this use.

Venous malformation

Venous malformation is a malformation of veins that appears as a soft, bluish, compressible swelling of the skin, mucosa, muscle, or deeper tissues. Symptoms include pain (often from small clots forming inside the lesion), swelling that changes with posture, and, in large lesions, abnormalities of blood clotting. Treatments include compression garments, sclerotherapy, surgery, anticoagulation for clotting problems, and drug therapy such as sirolimus for intractable cases. Blue rubber bleb nevus syndrome, with multiple venous malformations of the skin and gastrointestinal tract, is a rare related condition.

Arteriovenous malformation

Arteriovenous malformation (AVM) is a high-flow malformation in which arteries connect directly to veins without a normal capillary network. Lesions may be warm and pulsating and can progress over time, causing pain, ulceration, bleeding, and — in severe cases — heart failure due to the high blood flow. Treatment usually combines embolization and surgery at experienced centers. Because AVMs behave differently from slow-flow malformations, accurate diagnosis of the flow type is particularly important.

Combined vascular malformations and related syndromes

Some patients have combinations of capillary, lymphatic, and venous malformations, often together with overgrowth of the affected limb or region. Examples include Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome (capillary–lymphatic–venous malformation with limb overgrowth) and CLOVES syndrome. Many of these conditions are caused by somatic gene variants in the PIK3CA/AKT/mTOR or RAS/MAPK signaling pathways — a discovery that underpins the use of molecular targeted drugs such as sirolimus.

Note: The descriptions on this page are simplified for general readers. Presentation and treatment differ from patient to patient — please consult specialists in vascular anomalies for diagnosis and treatment decisions.
